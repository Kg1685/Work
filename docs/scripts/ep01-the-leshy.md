## 0. Первые 15 секунд (хук, без приветствия)

> In the forests of northern Russia, there is a rule older than the church, older than the roads, older than the names of the villages. // When the light fails, you do not call out. // You do not answer. // And you never, ever whistle.

*[эмбиент: ветер, далёкий костёр]*

> Tonight we go into those woods. We will start with the rules your great-grandmother knew by heart. Then the stories that were told to keep people alive. // And then — the one thing the rules were written to prevent.

---

## 1. Кто он такой (2–3 мин)

> His name is *leshy*. The word comes from *les* — forest. Not a name, a title. The forest, made aware of itself. //

> He is not a demon and he is not a god. In the old belief, every place has a master: the house has the *domovoy*, the river has the *vodyanoy*, the swamp has the *bolotnik*. The forest — the largest thing in that world, the thing that begins where the road ends — has him. //

> Ethnographers who went out with notebooks in the 1800s did not treat him as a fairy tale. Alexander Afanasyev collected thousands of tales in the 1850s and 60s. Sergey Maksimov walked the northern provinces. Dmitry Zelenin, working into the 1920s, catalogued the beliefs themselves — who was feared, by whom, and in which district. // Peasants discussed the leshy the way they discussed weather and wolves: as a fact you plan around. //

> And the plan was simple. You do not own the forest. You are a guest. Guests are polite.

---

## 2. Правила (4–5 мин) — сердце ролика, самое «цитируемое»

> **Rule one: do not whistle.** In the Arkhangelsk province they said it plainly: the only thing that whistles in the forest is the leshy. A man who whistles is speaking a language that is not his. // In some accounts, he will follow you home. //

> **Rule two: do not call out.** Not for a friend, not for a dog, not for fun. And if something calls *you* — by your name, in a voice you love — you do not answer. //

> **Rule three: do not sleep on the road.** The crossing of two paths was his. Travelers were warned never to stop for the night where roads meet. If you had to shelter in a forest hut, you asked permission first — out loud, before you went in. //

> **Rule four: do not boast, do not curse, do not make noise.** Loud children were said to annoy him. There is something in the records that is almost funny: the great forest spirit, offended by bad manners. //

> **Rule five: if you are lost, turn your clothes inside out.** Put your boots on the wrong feet. Left becomes right, and the spell breaks. // It sounds like nonsense until you notice what it is: a ritual of reversal. In a world where everything has a correct order, you step outside of it. You stop being the person he is leading. //

> And rule six, the practical one, the one that survives in hunting camps to this day: **bring something.** Bread, salt, a coin on a stump. *Master of the forest, forgive me, let me pass.* A shepherd who wanted his herd safe for a season came to the edge of the wood, took off his cross, and made a contract — with terms. No picking mushrooms. No killing, even in self-defence. Never say his name aloud. // A contract with the forest, in the 1800s, in writing? No. In bread and salt.

---

## 3. История: что рассказывали (5–7 мин)

> Here is where the folktale and the field record separate, and it matters. //

> In the tales, he is a giant: head above the treetops in the deep forest, no taller than the grass in a clearing. He wears his right boot on his left foot, and his coat the wrong way round, and his beard is combed to the left. He can be a bear, a wolf, an old man, a whirlwind, a mushroom. // In the field records, he is more often *a sound*. Laughter that makes the forest groan. A voice that barks like a dog, cries like a child, neighs like a horse. // Something that knows your name. //

> One story, told across several provinces, is about a woodcutter. He is splitting a log when a hand — hairy, clawed — reaches into the crack to hold it open. The woodcutter pulls out the wedge. The hand is trapped. The leshy begs. The woodcutter, being a practical man, negotiates: safe passage out of the forest, no tricks, forever. The hand is freed. // The man gets home. // Years later he is in that forest again, and he forgets the deal, and he laughs at something, out loud. //

*[пауза 1,5 сек]*

> And that is the shape of every leshy story. Not a monster. A boundary. Cross it politely and you may never even know he was there.

---

## 4. Историческая подкладка (3–4 мин)

> Why would an entire rural culture build a legal system around a forest spirit? //

> Because the forest was not scenery. It was the economy. Timber. Charcoal. Pitch and tar. Honey and wax from wild hives — *bortnichestvo*, beekeeping in hollow trees, was a whole profession with its own laws. Furs, which were money before money. // The forest fed villages and it killed people. A man who got lost in a Russian forest in October did not simply take a longer walk home. //

> So the rules are not superstition. They are a safety protocol, dressed as a spirit: don't wander at night, don't split up, don't sleep on the crossing, mark your path, make noise so you can be found, and treat the place as if someone is watching — because, in practical terms, something is: the weather, the animals, the dark. //

> And note this: the leshy is described as *a master*, not a devil. The church, when it came, had to work to turn him into one. // In some tellings he is a fallen angel who landed in the trees; in others, a cursed man, or a child taken by the forest. There is a line from the Vladimir province that is one of the loneliest sentences in Russian folklore. He says of himself: *"I am a man like any other. I simply have no cross on me. My mother cursed me."*

## 5. История у костра (7–9 мин) — финальный акт

*Здесь начинается собственно «ночная история». Пишется от второго лица, настоящее время, короткие фразы.*

> Put yourself on that path. //

> It is late September. The birch leaves have gone the colour of old brass. You know this path because you have walked it since you were nine years old — out to the far pasture and back before dark. // Tonight you are late. The light is going. Somewhere to your left a bird calls once and then thinks better of it. //

> The path bends right. // It has always bent right. //

> You take ten more steps and the path bends right again. And then again. // Same birches. Same two stones where the ground dips. //

> You stop. You are not a child. You know how a person gets turned around. You take out your knife and you cut a mark in a trunk, and you walk on, and you count your steps, and you find your mark. //

> You are not afraid yet. That comes later. First comes the practical part of your mind, arranging things: the mark, the steps, the direction of the sunset, the moss on the north side. //

> Then the air goes quiet. Not silent — quiet. The wind you had stopped noticing is gone, and you notice it now because it is gone. //

*[пауза 2 сек, эмбиент почти исчезает]*

> And far off, to your left, someone laughs. //

> It is not a frightening laugh. That is the thing nobody warns you about. It is a low, easy laugh, the kind a man makes when he is sure of himself — and it goes on a little too long, and it comes from a place where no one should be standing. //

> You do the sensible thing. You walk faster, downhill, because downhill is the river, and the river is the village. // You walk for what feels like an hour. //

> The path bends right. //

> You find your mark in the trunk. //

> And now you are afraid, and your fear is a very old fear, and it has a very specific instruction attached to it, and you remember your grandmother's voice saying it, and you understand for the first time that she was not telling you a story. //

> Do not answer. //

> Because now you hear it. // Your own name. // In a voice you love more than your own. // From the middle of the trees, where that person cannot possibly be. //

*[пауза 3 сек]*

> And you do not answer. // You take off your coat and you put it on inside out. You swap your boots, left to right, right to left, and you feel ridiculous, and your hands are shaking. // And you walk. //

> In the morning, when they find you, you will be two kilometres from your own door, in a thicket you have walked past a thousand times. // And you will not be able to explain it. //

> And the only thing you will be sure of — the only thing you will say to the men who came out with lamps — is this: *I did not answer.*

## 6. Финал / CTA (60–90 сек)

> The leshy did not die with the villages. He moved. // He is in the Witcher, renamed. He is in the horror films. He is in the way a modern person, alone in a forest at dusk, will still, without knowing why, decide not to whistle. //

> My grandmother kept one rule from that list her whole life. She never whistled in the woods. She said it was rude. //

> Tomorrow night, the water. The *rusalka* — the drowned girl who sings on the bank — began her life in the old texts as something else entirely, and the story of how she changed is stranger than the story they tell now. //

> If you are listening by a fire tonight, do me one thing. // Do not answer. //

> Good night.
